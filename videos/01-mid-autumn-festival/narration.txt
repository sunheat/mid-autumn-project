Once upon a time in ancient China, there lived a legendary archer named Hou Yi and his wife, Chang'e.

Hou Yi was a hero. When ten blazing suns once scorched the Earth, he shot down nine of them and saved the people.

For this heroic deed, the Queen Mother of the West gave him an extraordinary reward: a magical medicine called the elixir of immortality.

Whoever drank it would become an immortal — a magical being who could live forever beyond the mortal world.

But there was enough for only one person.

If Hou Yi drank it and became immortal, he would have to leave the human world behind.

And that meant leaving Chang'e behind.

So he chose not to drink it.

Instead, he asked Chang'e to keep the elixir safe.

But one day, while Hou Yi was away, one of his apprentices, Pang Meng, tried to steal it.

Chang'e couldn’t let him have it.

With no time to lose, she made a desperate choice.

She drank it herself.

Almost at once, she began to rise.

The elixir had made her immortal! She could no longer remain in the human world.

Higher and higher she floated — above the house, above the mountains, and into the night sky.

But Chang'e still loved Hou Yi.

She wanted to stay as close to him as she could.

So she chose the Moon — the nearest place in the heavens to the human world below.

When Hou Yi returned and learned what had happened, he was heartbroken.

That night, he looked up at the bright full moon and thought of Chang'e.

He set out fruits and cakes beneath the moonlight, as if he could still share them with her from far away.

And that was how the Mid-Autumn Festival began.

Wait.

Is that really where the Mid-Autumn Festival came from?

Well...

That’s the legend.

Real history is a little messier.

Festivals aren’t always invented.

Sometimes, they grow.

Mid-Autumn grew from several old traditions that slowly came together.

For most of Chinese history, people’s lives depended heavily on farming.

They had to watch the seasons carefully — when to plant, when the rains were likely to come, and when crops were ready to harvest.

So autumn, the harvest season, naturally became a time to celebrate what had been gathered, perform seasonal rituals, and pray for good harvests to come.

But why the Moon?

Ancient Chinese people kept track of time by watching both the Sun and the Moon.

The traditional Chinese calendar followed both.

The Sun helped keep the calendar aligned with the seasons.

The Moon shaped the months.

Around the middle of each month, the Moon becomes full.

And long before the Mid-Autumn Festival existed in the form we know today, people in China were already holding autumn rituals to honour the Moon.

Over many centuries, harvest traditions, rituals honouring the Moon, moon-gazing, poetry, food, and stories like Chang'e gradually came together.

By the Tang and Song dynasties, something recognisably like today’s Mid-Autumn Festival had taken shape.

So no — the festival probably did not begin because Chang'e flew to the Moon.

But her story stayed, because it captured something people felt deeply.

And that feeling would become the heart of Mid-Autumn.

So what does Mid-Autumn actually look like today?

First, people come home.

More than anything else, Mid-Autumn is a festival of reunion.

And in Chinese culture, family is often thought of across generations, not just as one household.

That can mean grandparents, parents, grown-up children, brothers and sisters — sometimes aunts, uncles, and cousins too.

Several generations come back to the same table.

And once they do, a pattern starts to appear.

The same shape keeps showing up.

A full moon.

A round mooncake.

A family gathered in a circle around the dinner table.

In Chinese, yuan means round.

And roundness carries the idea of being whole, complete.

And that same yuan appears in tuanyuan — reunion:

people who belong together, together again.

A full moon is complete.

No piece is missing.

And a family feels complete in much the same way:

when no one is missing.

That is why the full moon became such a powerful symbol of reunion.

And that idea of reunion is everywhere in the festival.

Families sit down for a reunion dinner.

They cut and share round mooncakes.

And when night falls, they go outside — or simply look out of a window — to admire the full moon.

In many parts of China, Mid-Autumn also arrives with the scent of osmanthus — tiny golden flowers that bloom in autumn.

People admire them, flavour sweets and tea with them, and traditionally even make osmanthus wine.

And China is a big place, so different regions have traditions of their own.

In some parts of China, children may carry lanterns or solve lantern riddles.

In Shanghai, Mid-Autumn tables traditionally include duck, taro, and edamame.

And in Hong Kong, a great fire dragon dances through the streets in a blaze of light and incense.

But Mid-Autumn isn’t only about the people who made it home.

It is also about the ones who couldn’t.

For centuries, people have spent Mid-Autumn far away from those they love — away from parents, grandparents, children, or the place they think of as home.

And when that happens, people look up at the Moon.

Because somewhere far away, the people they love are under that same Moon.

The Moon becomes more than something beautiful in the sky.

It becomes a way to remember.

A way to miss someone.

A way to feel close across a great distance.

And maybe that is why the story of Chang'e never disappeared.

The legend probably did not create the festival.

But perhaps it stayed with the festival because, at its heart, it is also a story about separation —

and the wish to be together again.

Hou Yi on Earth.

Chang'e on the Moon.

Far apart, and still looking toward one another.

The full moon is whole because nothing is missing.

A family feels whole for the same reason.

And when someone is missing, we look up, remember them, and know that the same Moon shines over them too.

月圆，人团圆。

When the Moon is full, may the family be whole.

And wherever the people you love are, may you be together again soon.

Happy Mid-Autumn Festival.
